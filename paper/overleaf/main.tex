\begin{abstract}
A quadrotor can use a cable as a whip to accelerate its free tip
toward a target. Model-based planning of this motion must account
for both the quadrotor's tracking response and the resulting cable
deformation. We present AeroWhip, a sim-to-real-to-sim framework
for aerial cable whipping. The predictor connects a fitted vehicle response, offset attachment
geometry, and a reduced differentiable discrete elastic rod (DDER)
model. Learned acceleration corrections augment the vehicle and
cable dynamics.
An offline optimizer inspired by model predictive path integral
(MPPI) control plans an initial whipping maneuver. Physical
executions refine the vehicle and cable dynamics for command-to-tip
prediction and subsequent planning. We apply the updated model to
local command correction toward the original planned tip trajectory
at fixed times.
We evaluate prediction accuracy and the utility of the refined
model for physical reference tracking and target approach.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A rapid quadrotor maneuver can turn a hanging cable into a whip,
directing its free tip toward a target. The vehicle actuates the
attached end, and deformation along the cable produces the tip
motion. Ropes and cables already provide lightweight tools for
suspended-payload transport and aerial
grasping~\cite{sreenath2013trajectory,hitch}. Whipping requires
predicting how rapid vehicle commands produce cable deformation
and tip motion.

Whipping and dynamic cable manipulation have been studied through
model-based optimization with robotic
arms~\cite{zimmermann2021dynamic}, movement primitives in a
simulated human arm~\cite{nah2023learning}, and learning-based
control~\cite{wang2024learning}. Aerial whipping adds the challenge
of maintaining flight while driving the cable. A quadrotor changes
its attitude to accelerate horizontally, so both translation and
rotation move an offset attachment. Tracking errors during rapid
flight further alter the executed motion~\cite{torrente2021data}.
Predicting the attachment trajectory therefore requires a model
of the vehicle's response to commands.

Aerial cable research provides foundations for this prediction.
Suspended-payload studies address trajectory
generation~\cite{sreenath2013trajectory}, flexible-cable
dynamics~\cite{8308004}, agile flight~\cite{cao2026flare}, and
adaptation to changes in flight dynamics~\cite{belkhale2021model}.
Work with free-ended cables has demonstrated parameter
identification and dynamic shape control using a single
quadrotor~\cite{shen2025aerial}, while related research combines
identification from measured endpoint motion with cable manipulation
using multiple UAVs~\cite{gabellieri2025manipulation}.
For whipping, the predictor must connect the vehicle's tracking
response to cable deformation and the resulting free-tip trajectory.

Rod mechanics provides a basis for predicting that deformation.
Discrete elastic rods represent distributed bending and
twisting~\cite{bergou2008discrete}, and DEFORM combines
differentiable rod mechanics with learning for cable prediction,
tracking, and manipulation~\cite{pmlr-v270-chen25d}.
Related studies investigate physical parameter
identification~\cite{chen2025accurate} and structured dynamics
learning from limited measurements~\cite{mamedov2025learning}.
These approaches motivate a cable predictor that retains rod
mechanics and learns corrections from measured motion.

Execution measurements can improve subsequent behavior by
updating actions or the model used to plan them. Iterative
Residual Policy refines actions from previous
executions~\cite{chi2024iterative}. Suresh and
Atkeson~\cite{suresh2026learning} use task-level iterative learning
control for dynamic rope manipulation, mapping measured task errors
to command updates through a local inverse model of the robot and rope.
Model-based iterative learning has also improved quadrotor
tracking by updating feedforward commands across
repetitions~\cite{schoellig2012optimization}.
Real2Sim2Real uses cable observations to improve simulation and
subsequent casting actions~\cite{lim2022real2sim2real}, while
Wiggle and Go identifies rope properties through a probing motion
before planning dynamic tasks~\cite{jakobsson2026wiggle}.
Sampling-based planning has also been combined with repeated
fitting of learned dynamics~\cite{williams2017information}.
AeroWhip refines the vehicle and cable dynamics from measured
motion, yielding an updated command-to-tip model for prediction
and planning. Fixed-reference command correction provides one
application of this model.

We present \emph{AeroWhip}, a sim-to-real-to-sim framework for
aerial cable whipping through iterative model refinement. Its predictor combines vehicle
response, offset attachment geometry, and reduced rod mechanics
with learned acceleration corrections. An offline optimizer
inspired by model predictive path integral (MPPI)
control~\cite{williams2017model} balances cable-shape agreement,
directed tip motion, and target approach under motion constraints.
Physical executions refine both the vehicle and cable models.
These updates support command-to-tip prediction and subsequent
planning. Here, local command correction uses the updated predictor
to reproduce the original planned tip motion at fixed times.
We evaluate prediction accuracy and whether model-guided correction
improves physical reference tracking and target approach.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem_statement}

A quadrotor carries a cable of length $L>0$, attached at one end
and free at the other. The attachment point is fixed relative
to the vehicle, and the cable is modeled as freely pivoting there.
We assume uniform material properties, negligible axial extension,
a naturally straight and untwisted undeformed configuration,
and isotropic bending stiffness.
Planning starts from nominal hover with the cable hanging at rest.

The task is to bring the free tip close to a stationary target
$\mathbf p_{\mathrm{tar}}\in\mathbb R^3$ with rapid motion along
a prescribed horizontal unit direction $\mathbf e\in\mathbb R^3$.
The strike direction specifies the desired tip motion at the
target, independently of the target's position relative to launch.
Let $t=0$ denote maneuver onset and $T>0$ the targeting horizon.
The planner generates desired quadrotor position, velocity,
acceleration, and yaw trajectories, which are fixed before
execution and tracked by the existing flight controller.

Figure~\ref{fig:problem_whipping} illustrates the whipping
motion considered in AeroWhip. A forward and upward pull is
followed by retraction, during which a bend travels toward the
free end as the cable straightens and the tip approaches the target.
This schematic and travelling-deformation descriptions of
whipping~\cite{MCMILLEN2003192} motivate characterizing the
motion through both cable-shape evolution and tip kinematics.

Let $\mathbf r(s,t)\in\mathbb R^3$ denote the world-frame position
of the cable material point at unstretched distance $sL$ from the
attachment, with $s\in[0,1]$. At a fixed time $t$, varying $s$
traces the centerline $\mathbf r(\cdot,t)$; fixing $s$ follows
the same material point throughout the maneuver.
The attachment and tip positions are
$\mathbf p_a(t)=\mathbf r(0,t)$ and
$\mathbf r_{\mathrm{tip}}(t)=\mathbf r(1,t)$, respectively,
with tip velocity
$\mathbf v_{\mathrm{tip}}(t)=\dot{\mathbf r}_{\mathrm{tip}}(t)$.

We compare predicted and reference shapes at the same
$M\geq3$ material coordinates
$s_j=j/(M-1)$, $j=0,\ldots,M-1$.
These samples are equally spaced along the unstretched cable
and need not coincide with measurement markers.
The unit direction between neighboring samples is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf d_j(t)
=
\frac{\mathbf r(s_{j+1},t)-\mathbf r(s_j,t)}
{\|\mathbf r(s_{j+1},t)-\mathbf r(s_j,t)\|_2},
\label{eq:whip_segment_direction}
\end{equation}
for $j=0,\ldots,M-2$, assuming distinct neighboring positions.
Sampled positions describe the centerline, and changes between
neighboring directions describe bending. Their histories support
the planner's reference-shape comparison.

Physical target approach is measured by the minimum
three-dimensional tip-to-target distance:
\begin{equation}
d_{\min}
=\min_{t\in[0,T]}
\|\mathbf r_{\mathrm{tip}}(t)-\mathbf p_{\mathrm{tar}}\|_2.
\label{eq:target_distance}
\end{equation}
For physical evaluation, we substitute the measured trajectory
$\mathbf r_{\mathrm{tip}}^{\mathrm{meas}}$ into~\eqref{eq:target_distance}.
Piecewise-linear interpolation within valid tracking intervals
estimates the minimum; tracking gaps are excluded and reported.
Tip velocity describes approach speed and alignment with
$\mathbf e$. Initial planning uses tip kinematics and shape
agreement; subsequent correction tracks the resulting planned
tip trajectory (Section~\ref{subsec:offline_planning}).

\subsection{DDER Cable Model}
\label{subsec:dder_model}

We represent the cable with a reduced differentiable discrete
elastic rod (DDER) model based on the rod mechanics of
Bergou et al.~\cite{bergou2008discrete}.

As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:problem_dder}, we discretize
the centerline using $N\geq3$ simulation nodes with
positions $\mathbf r_i(t)\in\mathbb R^3$ and velocities
$\mathbf v_i(t)\in\mathbb R^3$, $i=0,\ldots,N-1$.
Nodes follow fixed material locations. The vectors
$\mathbf r(t),\mathbf v(t)\in\mathbb R^{3N}$ stack their positions
and velocities, respectively.
Node $0$ follows the prescribed attachment,
$\mathbf r_0=\mathbf p_a$, and node $N-1$ is the free tip,
$\mathbf r_{N-1}=\mathbf r_{\mathrm{tip}}$.

Segment $i$ connects nodes $i$ and $i+1$ and has fixed length
$\ell_i>0$. Inextensibility imposes
\[
\|\mathbf r_{i+1}-\mathbf r_i\|_2=\ell_i,
\qquad
\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\ell_i=L.
\]
Linear interpolation between nodes defines the model centerline
$\mathbf r(s,t)$ and the material samples for shape comparison.
The simulation-node count $N$ and shape-sample count $M$ need
not be equal.

Material twist is the relative rotation of cable cross-sections
about the centerline. We assume these orientations remain at
elastic equilibrium, with no imposed end twist or twisting moment.
For the naturally straight, untwisted, isotropic rod, this gives
zero material twist~\cite{bergou2008discrete}. We omit independent
twist coordinates and torsional energy while retaining
three-dimensional centerline dynamics.

Bending energy penalizes changes in direction between adjacent
simulation segments. Define the unit segment tangent
\[
\mathbf t_i
=\frac{\mathbf r_{i+1}-\mathbf r_i}
{\|\mathbf r_{i+1}-\mathbf r_i\|_2}.
\]
Tangents $\mathbf t_i$ describe model segments; directions
$\mathbf d_j$ in~\eqref{eq:whip_segment_direction} describe
shape-sampling intervals.
For each interior node $i=1,\ldots,N-2$, let
$\bar\ell_i=(\ell_{i-1}+\ell_i)/2$ be the average rest length
of its adjacent segments.
The discrete curvature binormal and bending energy are
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\kappa_i^b
=\frac{2\,\mathbf t_{i-1}\times\mathbf t_i}
{1+\mathbf t_{i-1}^\top\mathbf t_i},
\qquad
E_b(\mathbf r)=\frac{EI}{2}
\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}\frac{\|\boldsymbol\kappa_i^b\|_2^2}{\bar\ell_i},
\label{eq:bending_energy}
\end{equation}
where $EI>0$ is the bending stiffness.
The dimensionless curvature binormal measures turning between
adjacent segments and, when nonzero, is normal to their plane.
It vanishes for aligned tangents and is singular for
antiparallel tangents.

The ideal bending force is
$\mathbf f_b=-\nabla_{\mathbf r}E_b$.
Numerical evaluation uses a positive denominator floor near
antiparallel tangents.
Time integration, constraint enforcement, and learned
acceleration corrections are described in
Section~\ref{subsec:prediction_model}.

Admissible commands satisfy prescribed motion bounds and pass
simulation checks on vehicle altitude, cable ground clearance,
and numerical validity using the current predictor.
Section~\ref{subsec:offline_planning} specifies these conditions.

\noindent\textbf{Problem (Aerial Cable Whipping).}
Given an initial hovering configuration, target
$\mathbf p_{\mathrm{tar}}$, strike direction $\mathbf e$,
horizon $T$, and a reference shape history, generate admissible
quadrotor commands that bring the tip close to the target through
the desired whipping motion.
Use physical executions to refine the predictor and correct
commands toward the selected motion. The problem comprises
three connected subproblems.

\noindent\textbf{Subproblem 1 (Command-to-Tip Prediction).}
Predict cable motion from desired quadrotor commands, accounting
for vehicle response, attachment geometry, and cable dynamics.

\noindent\textbf{Subproblem 2 (Offline Trajectory Optimization).}
Plan an admissible whipping command and, after model refinement,
correct it toward the original predicted tip trajectory.

\noindent\textbf{Subproblem 3 (Model Refinement).}
Update the predictor using recorded commands and measured vehicle
and cable motion to support command correction and subsequent planning.



\section{AeroWhip Framework}
\label{sec:aerowhip_framework}

AeroWhip combines command-to-tip prediction, offline trajectory
optimization, and model refinement. An initial MPPI plan
defines the desired whipping motion. Physical executions supply
measurements for refining the vehicle and cable models, which then
guide local command correction toward that motion. The refined
predictor also remains available for subsequent simulation and
planning. Below, we describe the predictor, the two command
optimization stages, and the model-fitting procedure.

\subsection{Quadrotor and Cable Prediction Model}
\label{subsec:prediction_model}

The predictor maps commands to vehicle motion, then uses the
resulting attachment trajectory to drive the cable model.
The fitted vehicle response approximates cable-loading effects
under the observed motion conditions; cable configuration and reaction
forces are not explicit inputs. Vehicle and cable states below
denote predictions unless marked as measured.

The inputs are desired position $\mathbf p_d$, velocity
$\mathbf v_d$, acceleration $\mathbf a_d$, and yaw $\psi_d$,
held constant between command updates. Let $\mathbf p_q$
and $\mathbf v_q$ denote the position and velocity of the
vehicle's tracked origin, and let $\mathbf R\in\mathrm{SO}(3)$
map vectors from the vehicle's body-fixed tracking frame to
the world frame.
We approximate the closed-loop translational response by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf a_{\mathrm{nom}}(t)
={}&\mathbf K_p[\mathbf p_d(t-\tau_d)-\mathbf p_q(t)]\\
&+\mathbf K_v[\mathbf v_d(t-\tau_d)-\mathbf v_q(t)]\\
&+\mathbf K_a\mathbf a_d(t-\tau_d)+\mathbf b_q,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:quadrotor_response}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_d\geq0$ is an effective delay and $\mathbf b_q$
is a constant acceleration bias within each rollout.
The fitted diagonal gains $\mathbf K_p$, $\mathbf K_v$, and
$\mathbf K_a$ each share a horizontal coefficient and have a
separate vertical coefficient. Translation satisfies
$\dot{\mathbf p}_q=\mathbf v_q$ and
$\dot{\mathbf v}_q=\mathbf a_{\mathrm{nom}}+\Delta\mathbf a_q$,
where $\Delta\mathbf a_q$ is a learned acceleration correction.

Vehicle attitude determines the rotation of the offset attachment.
We construct a commanded rotation $\mathbf R_c$ with its third
axis parallel to
$\mathbf S\mathbf a_{\mathrm{nom}}+g\mathbf e_3$,
where $g>0$ is gravitational acceleration,
$\mathbf e_3=(0,0,1)^\top$ is the upward world direction,
and $\mathbf S$ is a fitted positive diagonal matrix with
dimensionless horizontal and vertical scales.
The first axis is the normalized projection of the delayed yaw
heading $\psi_d(t-\tau_d)$ onto the plane normal to the third
axis; the second completes a right-handed frame. Zero specific
force or a parallel heading makes this construction invalid.

An effective alignment $\mathbf Q\in\mathrm{SO}(3)$, fixed
during each prediction, maps tracking-frame coordinates to the
vehicle-axis convention used for $\mathbf R_c$, giving the
desired orientation of the tracking frame,
$\mathbf R_d=\mathbf R_c\mathbf Q$.
Let $\mathbf e_R$ be the principal rotation vector of
$\mathbf R^\top\mathbf R_d$, expressed in the tracking frame.
The attitude response is
\begin{equation}
\dot{\mathbf R}=\mathbf R[\boldsymbol\omega]_\times,
\qquad
\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}=\frac{\mathbf e_R}{\tau_R^2}
-\frac{2\boldsymbol\omega}{\tau_R},
\label{eq:attitude_response}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol\omega$ is tracking-frame angular velocity,
$\tau_R>0$ is a fitted response timescale, and
$[\boldsymbol\omega]_\times$ denotes the cross-product matrix.
The commanded orientation uses nominal acceleration;
$\Delta\mathbf a_q$ affects later attitude predictions
through the evolving position and velocity errors.

For a fixed tracking-frame offset $\boldsymbol\rho_a$
from the tracked origin to the attachment, the attachment
position is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf p_a=\mathbf p_q+\mathbf R\boldsymbol\rho_a.
\label{eq:attachment_mapping}
\end{equation}
This trajectory prescribes the moving cable root,
$\mathbf r_0=\mathbf p_a$, with root velocity obtained from
finite differences of the interpolated positions.

For each free node $i=1,\ldots,N-1$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
m_i\dot{\mathbf v}_i
={}&\mathbf f_{b,i}
   +\mathbf f_{\mathrm{bd},i}
   +\mathbf f_{\mathrm{con},i}\\
&-m_i\gamma_c\mathbf v_i
 -m_i g\mathbf e_3
 +m_i\Delta\mathbf a_{c,i},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:driven_cable_dynamics}
\end{equation}
with $\dot{\mathbf r}_i=\mathbf v_i$.
Here $m_i$ is the node mass, $\mathbf f_{b,i}$ is the bending
force from~\eqref{eq:bending_energy}, and
$\mathbf f_{\mathrm{bd},i}$ is the bending-damping force.
The reaction $\mathbf f_{\mathrm{con},i}$ enforces fixed
segment lengths, $\gamma_c\geq0$ is the linear-drag rate,
and $\Delta\mathbf a_{c,i}$ is a learned acceleration correction.

Bending damping uses a regularized curvature rate that
approximately removes the contribution of local rigid rotation.
At each interior node $i=1,\ldots,N-2$, define
\[
\mathbf n_i=\mathbf t_{i-1}\times\mathbf t_i,
\qquad
c_i=\mathbf t_{i-1}^\top\mathbf t_i,
\qquad
\alpha_i=
\frac{\varepsilon}{\|\mathbf n_i\|_2^2+\varepsilon},
\]
where $\varepsilon>0$ is a fixed dimensionless regularization
parameter.
Using the curvature binormal
$\boldsymbol\kappa_i^b$ from~\eqref{eq:bending_energy},
the local angular-velocity estimate and regularized bending
rate operator are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol\omega_i^{\mathrm{loc}}
&=\tfrac12(\mathbf I-\mathbf t_{i-1}\mathbf t_{i-1}^\top)
(\mathbf t_{i-1}\times\dot{\mathbf t}_{i-1}
+\mathbf t_i\times\dot{\mathbf t}_i),\\
\dot{\boldsymbol\kappa}_{i,\mathrm{rel}}^b
&=-\frac{2\mathbf n_i\dot c_i}
{(1+c_i)(\|\mathbf n_i\|_2^2+\varepsilon)}\\
&\quad+\alpha_i\bigl(\dot{\boldsymbol\kappa}_i^b
-\boldsymbol\omega_i^{\mathrm{loc}}\times\boldsymbol\kappa_i^b\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:regularized_bending_rate}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf I$ is the $3\times3$ identity matrix, and dots
denote time derivatives induced by node velocities.
The regularization keeps the rate finite when adjacent tangents
point in the same direction.

The dissipation function and stacked bending-damping force are
\begin{equation}
\mathcal D_b=\frac{C_b}{2}
\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}\frac{\|\dot{\boldsymbol\kappa}_{i,\mathrm{rel}}^b\|_2^2}{\bar\ell_i},
\qquad
\mathbf f_{\mathrm{bd}}=-\nabla_{\mathbf v}\mathcal D_b,
\label{eq:bending_damping}
\end{equation}
where $C_b\geq0$ is the bending-damping coefficient.
The cable state is advanced with implicit bending damping and
mass-weighted position and velocity projections for the
inextensibility constraints.

Two multilayer perceptrons provide the learned acceleration
corrections. The vehicle network takes the position and velocity
tracking errors in~\eqref{eq:quadrotor_response}, delayed desired
acceleration, predicted velocity, and bias $\mathbf b_q$,
and outputs $\Delta\mathbf a_q$.
The cable network takes all node positions and velocities
relative to the attachment,
$\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_0$ and $\mathbf v_i-\mathbf v_0$,
together with attachment velocity $\mathbf v_0$.
It jointly outputs $\Delta\mathbf a_{c,i}$ for all free nodes;
the prescribed root receives no correction.

All vector inputs and outputs use world-frame components.
Inputs receive fixed scaling, and a scaled hyperbolic tangent
bounds each correction component by $a_{\max}>0$.
A motion-dependent factor suppresses the vehicle correction at
rest and increases toward one with the magnitudes of predicted
velocity, delayed desired velocity, and delayed desired acceleration.
Network weights remain fixed during each rollout and are fitted
as described in Section~\ref{subsec:model_refinement}.

Planning and preflight forecasts start from nominal hover,
with the tracking frame and commanded heading aligned with the
world axes: $\mathbf R(0)=\mathbf Q=\mathbf I$ and
$\mathbf b_q=\mathbf0$. Initial vehicle, cable, and angular
velocities are zero, and the cable hangs straight.
Before maneuver onset, commands hold the initial desired
position with zero desired velocity and acceleration.
Fitting and postflight model comparisons initialize the state,
bias, and alignment from pre-maneuver measurements. These
comparisons are distinct from the saved preflight forecasts.

\subsection{Offline Trajectory Optimization}
\label{subsec:offline_planning}

Initial planning uses MPPI-inspired sampling to select a whipping
motion. After model refinement, local optimization corrects commands
toward its predicted tip trajectory. Both stages use the same
B-spline representation and hold model parameters fixed.

We represent the desired vehicle position by a smooth command spline
using a fixed clamped quintic B-spline basis:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf p_s(t;\boldsymbol\theta)
=\sum_{j=0}^{n_c-1}B_j(t)\mathbf p_j^{\mathrm{ctrl}},
\qquad t\in[0,T].
\label{eq:spline_reference}
\end{equation}
Here $B_j$ are the basis functions and $\mathbf p_j^{\mathrm{ctrl}}\in\mathbb R^3$
are the $n_c\geq6$ control points. We fix
$\mathbf p_0^{\mathrm{ctrl}}=\mathbf p_1^{\mathrm{ctrl}}=\mathbf p_2^{\mathrm{ctrl}}=\mathbf p_q(0)$, giving the
initial position and zero initial velocity and acceleration.
The remaining control points form
$\boldsymbol\theta\in\mathbb R^{3(n_c-3)}$.
Desired position, velocity, and acceleration are sampled from
$\mathbf p_s$, $\dot{\mathbf p}_s$, and $\ddot{\mathbf p}_s$,
respectively, and held between command updates. Desired yaw
remains constant. The spline end is free to support follow-through.

Initial planning fits the spline to a selected seed command under
jerk bounds. Candidate rollouts must satisfy bounds on spline jerk,
sampled command speed and height, and the magnitude, tilt, and
vertical component of $\mathbf a_d+g\mathbf e_3$.
Admissibility also requires predicted vehicle-height limits,
cable ground clearance, and numerical validity.

For initial whip design, the optimizer maximizes
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
J(\boldsymbol\theta)
={}&w_{\mathrm{whip}}S_{\mathrm{tar}}S_{\mathrm{whip}}
+w_{\mathrm{enc}}S_{\mathrm{enc}}+w_{\mathrm{ext}}S_{\mathrm{ext}}\\
&-w_{\mathrm{miss}}(1-S_{\mathrm{tar}})
-C_{\mathrm{motion}}-C_{\mathrm{exit}},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:planning_score}
\end{equation}
where $S_{\mathrm{tar}}$ measures target proximity and $S_{\mathrm{whip}}$
measures reference-shape agreement. The scores $S_{\mathrm{enc}}$
and $S_{\mathrm{ext}}$ favor vehicle retraction and directed tip
extension, while $C_{\mathrm{motion}}$ and $C_{\mathrm{exit}}$
penalize vehicle motion and recovery handover conditions.
The four score weights are positive; all terms are defined below
and evaluated on the candidate rollout.

\noindent\emph{Target approach and directed motion.}
A sphere of radius $r_{\mathrm{tar}}>0$ centered at
$\mathbf p_{\mathrm{tar}}$ defines the planning tolerance region.
The encounter time $t_e$ is the first entry by any cable node
over $[0,T]$; if none enters, it is the time of closest tip
approach. Entry is evaluated along interpolated node trajectories;
the tolerance sphere does not exert collision forces. With
$d_e=\|\mathbf r_{\mathrm{tip}}(t_e)-\mathbf p_{\mathrm{tar}}\|_2$,
the proximity score is $S_{\mathrm{tar}}=\exp[-(d_e/\sigma_p)^2]$ for
$\sigma_p>0$. This encounter-based distance differs from the
physical minimum-distance metric in~\eqref{eq:target_distance}.

At $t_e$, let $v_{\mathrm{tip},\parallel}=\mathbf e^\top\mathbf v_{\mathrm{tip}}$
and $v_{q,\parallel}=\mathbf e^\top\mathbf v_q$ denote the forward
tip and vehicle velocities. Let $b_e$ be the vehicle's retraction
from its greatest preceding forward position, and let
$\ell_e=\mathbf e^\top(\mathbf r_{\mathrm{tip}}-\mathbf p_a)$
be the forward attachment-to-tip extension. The indicator $\chi$
is one when no node enters the sphere or when the tip enters
strictly before every other node, and zero otherwise.
Define $\operatorname{clip}(z)=\min(1,\max(0,z))$ and
$h(z)=h_{\min}+(1-h_{\min})\operatorname{clip}(z)$, with a fixed
floor $0<h_{\min}<1$. Then
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
S_{\mathrm{enc}}
&=S_{\mathrm{tar}}\chi\,h(v_{\mathrm{tip},\parallel}/v_{\mathrm{tip},s})
h(-v_{q,\parallel}/v_{q,s})h(b_e/b_s),\\
S_{\mathrm{ext}}
&=S_{\mathrm{tar}}\chi\,\operatorname{clip}(v_{\mathrm{tip},\parallel}/v_{\mathrm{tip},s})
\operatorname{clip}(\ell_e/L)\\
&\quad\times\operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(
v_{\mathrm{tip},\parallel}/\|\mathbf v_{\mathrm{tip}}\|_2\bigr)^2.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:directed_motion_scores}
\end{equation}
The positive scales $v_{\mathrm{tip},s}$, $v_{q,s}$, and $b_s$
set the saturation levels. The alignment factor favors the strike
direction and is defined as zero at zero tip speed. The positive
floor in $h$ makes vehicle reversal a preference rather than a
requirement.

\noindent\emph{Whipping-shape agreement.}
An earlier simulated whip supplies the reference shape history,
denoted by the superscript $\mathrm{ref}$. It was generated with a
different development model and guides initial design alongside
the seed command.

The normalized samples
$\bar{\mathbf r}_j(t)=[\mathbf r(s_j,t)-\mathbf p_a(t)]/L$
remove attachment translation and cable-length scale while
retaining world orientation. Apply the same normalization to the
reference using its own attachment position and cable length.
Shape comparison uses these samples and the directions
$\mathbf d_j(t)$ in~\eqref{eq:whip_segment_direction}.

We align encounter times and allow limited intermediate retiming.
For $K\geq2$ phases $u_k=k/(K-1)$, $k=0,\ldots,K-1$,
define paired times $t_k^{\mathrm{ref}}=t_e^{\mathrm{ref}}u_k$
and $t_k^\gamma=t_eu_k^\gamma$. Here $t_e^{\mathrm{ref}}>0$ is the
reference encounter time, and $\gamma$ belongs to a fixed finite
set $\Gamma$ of positive values containing $1$.
The shape score is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
S_{\mathrm{whip}}&=\max_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\exp\Bigl[
-\frac{\bigl\langle\|\bar{\mathbf r}_j(t_k^\gamma)
-\bar{\mathbf r}_j^{\mathrm{ref}}(t_k^{\mathrm{ref}})\|_2^2\bigr\rangle_{j,k}}{\sigma_r^2}\\
&\quad-\frac{\bigl\langle\|\mathbf d_j(t_k^\gamma)
-\mathbf d_j^{\mathrm{ref}}(t_k^{\mathrm{ref}})\|_2^2\bigr\rangle_{j,k}}{\sigma_d^2}
-\beta|\log\gamma|\Bigr].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:whip_similarity}
\end{equation}
The brackets average over $k=0,\ldots,K-1$ and
$j=0,\ldots,M-1$ for positions or $j=0,\ldots,M-2$ for
directions, pairing the same material locations. The positive
scales $\sigma_r,\sigma_d$ are dimensionless, and $\beta\geq0$
penalizes departure from uniform phase sampling. We set
$S_{\mathrm{whip}}=0$ when $t_e=0$.
This score compares deformation histories; the horizon,
directed-speed rewards, and command constraints govern physical timing.

The motion cost $C_{\mathrm{motion}}$ accumulates time-weighted
squared vehicle displacements from launch and command-spline jerk.
Jerk is normalized by its componentwise bounds. Displacement penalties use
positive forward motion and lateral motion along
$\mathbf e_3\times\mathbf e$. Accumulation ends at first tip
entry, or at $T$ if none occurs. Recovery begins at the command
boundary ending that entry interval, or at $T$.
At handover, $C_{\mathrm{exit}}$ penalizes weighted squares of
command speed, positive upward velocity, and positive acceleration
along the command velocity; the last term is zero at zero speed.
Infeasible candidates receive $J=-\infty$.

\noindent\emph{Initial planning by sampling.}
An offline MPPI-inspired search uses exponential trajectory
weighting~\cite{williams2017model} and maintains several proposal
means. For each mean $\boldsymbol\mu$, it samples
$\boldsymbol\theta^{(b)}=\boldsymbol\mu+\boldsymbol\epsilon^{(b)}$.
The perturbations follow a fixed mixture of correlated Gaussian
distributions at several scales. Their covariance favors coherent
variations of neighboring control points while exploring all
free coordinates. Candidates are simulated in parallel.

Let $\mathcal V$ index a proposal's feasible random samples and
$J_b=J(\boldsymbol\theta^{(b)})$. For nonempty $\mathcal V$,
\begin{equation}
w_b=\frac{\exp(J_b/\lambda)}{\sum_{a\in\mathcal V}\exp(J_a/\lambda)},
\qquad
\boldsymbol\mu^+=\sum_{b\in\mathcal V}w_b\boldsymbol\theta^{(b)}.
\label{eq:offline_sampling_update}
\end{equation}
Otherwise, the mean remains unchanged. The positive temperature
$\lambda$ is adjusted separately for each proposal to target a
prescribed effective sample count,
$(\sum_{b\in\mathcal V}w_b^2)^{-1}$.
Unperturbed proposal means and the best command found so far
are also evaluated; only random samples enter the weighted update.
The highest-scoring feasible candidate is retained across iterations.
Search ends at a score plateau or a fixed iteration budget.

\noindent\emph{Command correction with the refined model.}
Figure~\ref{fig:command_correction} shows the M0-to-M1 correction:
the fixed M1 model predicts motion under the original M0 commands
and the corrected M1 commands.
The desired tip trajectory and timing remain unchanged. The initial MPPI
plan's predicted tip and vehicle trajectories,
$\mathbf r_{\mathrm{tip}}^0(t)$ and $\mathbf p_q^0(t)$,
and position commands $\mathbf p_d^0(t)$ form the fixed reference.
Superscript $0$ identifies this plan; $\mathrm{ref}$ denotes
the earlier shape prior used only in initial planning.

Over the original plan's fixed handover interval $[0,T_h]$,
where $0<T_h\leq T$, we minimize
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
C_{\mathrm{track}}(\boldsymbol\theta)
={}&w_{\mathrm{tip}}\bigl\langle\|\mathbf r_{\mathrm{tip}}-\mathbf r_{\mathrm{tip}}^0\|_2^2\bigr\rangle
+w_q\bigl\langle\|\mathbf p_q-\mathbf p_q^0\|_2^2\bigr\rangle\\
&+w_d\bigl\langle\|\mathbf p_d-\mathbf p_d^0\|_2^2\bigr\rangle.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:reference_tracking_cost}
\end{equation}
Unmarked states are predictions under the refined model.
The brackets average squared distances at matching times over
$[0,T_h]$, using uniform prediction samples for the first two
terms and command samples for the third. Positive dimensionless
weights prioritize tip tracking, with smaller penalties for
vehicle-path deviation and changes from the original commands.
The reference, timing, objective, and admissibility constraints
remain fixed across correction rounds.

Starting from the latest executed command, regularized
Gauss--Newton updates adjust the spline controls affecting
$[0,T_h]$. Finite differences estimate tip and vehicle sensitivities;
command positions depend linearly on the controls. At each iteration,
we linearize the tracking errors and solve a constrained local
least-squares problem with bounded increments and quadratic
damping in scaled control coordinates. Backtracking accepts only
updates that reduce $C_{\mathrm{track}}$ in nonlinear simulation
and satisfy the command and rollout checks. Iteration ends at
small improvement, failure to find a feasible improving step,
or the computation budget.

Measurements affect command correction through model refinement,
described next. Both optimization stages verify the selected command
in an independent rollout, including a recovery with continuous
position, velocity, and acceleration.

\subsection{Model Refinement from Physical Executions}
\label{subsec:model_refinement}

The initial model $\mathcal M_0$ comes from preliminary calibration.
Each update starts from the preceding model and fits vehicle-response
parameters, cable coefficients, and learned acceleration corrections
to execution measurements. The fitting objective compares predicted and measured motion and does not
depend on the target or planned reference. Cable length, masses,
attachment geometry, and integration settings remain fixed.

Recordings contain the executed commands, measured vehicle pose,
and cable-marker positions. Equation~\eqref{eq:attachment_mapping}
maps measured vehicle pose to attachment motion. Each fitting
window starts from preceding measurements and advances without
resetting predicted states to later observations. Missing
observations are excluded from the loss.

The fitting set combines designated recordings from the latest
batch, earlier whipping batches, and preliminary motions.
Fixed group weights balance these conditions; within each group,
recordings have equal weight distributed among eligible windows.
Final test recordings remain outside fitting and model selection.

Vehicle fitting compares command-driven predictions with measured
vehicle motion. Cable fitting uses the measured attachment
trajectory, separating cable-dynamics errors from errors in
predicted vehicle motion. Both use a weighted simulation-error loss,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data}}
=\sum_{o\in\mathcal O}w_o\,\rho\!\left(\frac{\|\mathbf e_o\|_2^2}{\sigma_o^2}\right),
\label{eq:refinement_data_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal O$ contains valid observations,
$\mathbf e_o\in\mathbb R^3$ is the prediction error, and
$w_o\geq0$ with $\sum_{o\in\mathcal O}w_o=1$.
Position errors are predicted minus measured positions.
Orientation errors are the principal rotation vectors of
$\mathbf R_o^\top\mathbf R_o^{\mathrm{meas}}$, where
$\mathbf R_o$ and $\mathbf R_o^{\mathrm{meas}}$ are the
predicted and measured orientations.
Fixed positive scales $\sigma_o$ normalize position and
orientation errors. The robust penalty
$\rho(z)=2(\sqrt{1+z}-1)$ limits the influence of large errors.
Cable losses emphasize the tip while retaining the other observed
non-attachment markers.

The update proceeds in four stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Quadrotor response.}
    Fit $\mathbf K_p$, $\mathbf K_v$, $\mathbf K_a$, and
    $\tau_d$ using position errors, keeping the inherited vehicle
    correction fixed. Separately fit $\mathbf S$ and $\tau_R$
    using orientation errors.

    \item \emph{Quadrotor correction.}
    Fix the nominal translation parameters and fit the vehicle
    network using position errors over simulated trajectories.
    Then refit $\mathbf S$ and $\tau_R$: the corrected position
    and velocity predictions alter the nominal acceleration
    used to predict attitude.

    \item \emph{Cable physics.}
    Fit $EI$, $C_b$, and $\gamma_c$ using measured attachment
    motion and marker-position errors, keeping the inherited
    cable correction fixed.

    \item \emph{Cable correction.}
    Fix the fitted cable coefficients and fit the cable network
    using the same measured attachment trajectory and marker loss.
\end{enumerate}

Nominal parameter fits use fixed bounds and quadratic penalties
on changes from the initial values of each fit. Positive parameters
use logarithmic changes; the delay uses changes normalized by a
fixed time scale.

The neural stages penalize correction magnitude and deviation
from the inherited correction:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{NN}}
={}&\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data}}+\frac{\lambda_a}{3a_{\max}^2}\,
\Bigl\langle\|\Delta\mathbf a\|_2^2\\
&\quad+\|\Delta\mathbf a-\Delta\mathbf a^{\mathrm{prev}}\|_2^2\Bigr\rangle.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:refinement_neural_loss}
\end{equation}
Here $\Delta\mathbf a$ denotes the vehicle or free-node cable
correction, and $\Delta\mathbf a^{\mathrm{prev}}$ is the
inherited network evaluated at the same inputs.
The brackets average over weighted fitting windows and simulated
times, and additionally over free nodes for the cable. Cable
averages exclude times without valid marker observations.
The bound $a_{\max}$ normalizes acceleration, $\lambda_a\geq0$
sets regularization strength, and $3$ averages the components.
Training differentiates through complete rollouts and requires
no measured acceleration labels.

The retained $\mathcal M_0$ has no active cable correction.
The first update adds a network initialized to zero output, with
$\Delta\mathbf a^{\mathrm{prev}}=\mathbf0$ in
\eqref{eq:refinement_neural_loss}. This increases model capacity;
later updates inherit the weights and preserve the architecture.

Each fitting stage retains its best numerically valid training
iterate. Development recordings support model selection and
diagnostic comparisons; the selected models are frozen before
final evaluation. Complete command-to-tip prediction evaluates
the assembled model and is distinct from cable prediction driven
by measured attachment motion. It is not jointly optimized during
the staged fits.

The updated predictor returns to command correction or subsequent
MPPI planning. We evaluate prediction accuracy on held-out
executions and assess command correction through physical
reference tracking and target approach. Original commands and
their preflight forecasts are preserved across model updates.
