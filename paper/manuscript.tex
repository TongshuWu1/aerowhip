\begin{abstract}
A quadrotor can use a cable as a whip to accelerate its free tip
toward a target. Model-based planning of this motion must account
for both the quadrotor's tracking response and the resulting cable
deformation. We present AeroWhip, a sim-to-real-to-sim framework
for aerial cable whipping. Its prediction model connects desired
quadrotor commands to cable motion through a fitted vehicle
response, attachment geometry, and a reduced differentiable discrete
elastic rod (DDER) model with learned acceleration corrections.
An offline optimizer inspired by model predictive path integral
(MPPI) control plans each maneuver. After execution, we use the
recorded vehicle and cable motion to refine the predictor before
planning the next maneuver. The existing flight controller tracks
the desired trajectory. We evaluate physical target approach using
the minimum three-dimensional tip-to-target distance, and prediction
accuracy by comparing cable-tip trajectories predicted from desired
quadrotor commands with measured trajectories.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Ropes and cables provide aerial robots with lightweight and flexible
tools for manipulation. They have been used to transport suspended
payloads and to form hitches for aerial
grasping~\cite{sreenath2013trajectory,hitch}. A cable can also serve
as a whip, allowing a robot to direct its free tip toward a target
through a rapid motion of the attached end. We study aerial cable
whipping, in which a quadrotor generates this motion, and use
measurements from physical executions to refine the models for
subsequent planning.

Arm-based studies of whipping and dynamic cable manipulation
include model-based trajectory
optimization~\cite{zimmermann2021dynamic}, movement primitives in a
simulated human arm~\cite{nah2023learning}, and learning-based
control~\cite{wang2024learning}. A quadrotor must maintain flight
while producing the motion needed for whipping. It changes its
attitude to accelerate horizontally, so both its position and
orientation affect an offset cable attachment. During rapid flight,
vehicle tracking errors change the attachment motion produced by
the desired trajectory~\cite{torrente2021data}. The planner must
therefore predict how desired quadrotor commands move the attachment
and, in turn, deform the cable.

Aerial cable research provides foundations for this problem.
Suspended-payload studies address trajectory
generation~\cite{sreenath2013trajectory}, flexible-cable
dynamics~\cite{8308004}, agile flight~\cite{cao2026flare}, and
adaptation to changes in flight dynamics~\cite{belkhale2021model}.
These tasks use the cable to transmit forces to a payload.
Cable parameter identification and dynamic shape control have also
been demonstrated with a single quadrotor carrying a free-ended
cable~\cite{shen2025aerial}. Related work combines identification
from measured endpoint motion with cable manipulation using
multiple UAVs~\cite{gabellieri2025manipulation}.
Our model-based planning approach predicts the free-tip trajectory
from desired vehicle commands, accounting for vehicle tracking
response as well as cable deformation.

Discrete elastic rods describe distributed bending and
twisting~\cite{bergou2008discrete}. DEFORM combines differentiable
rod mechanics with learning for cable prediction, tracking, and
manipulation~\cite{pmlr-v270-chen25d}. Other studies investigate
physical parameter identification~\cite{chen2025accurate} and
structured dynamics learning from limited
measurements~\cite{mamedov2025learning}. These approaches motivate
combining rod mechanics with corrections learned from observed
motion.

Measurements from physical executions can improve the next action
or the model used to plan it. Iterative Residual Policy and
task-level iterative learning control refine actions using
observations from previous
executions~\cite{chi2024iterative,suresh2026learning}.
Real2Sim2Real uses physical cable observations to improve simulation
and subsequent casting actions~\cite{lim2022real2sim2real}, while
Wiggle and Go identifies rope properties from a probing motion
before planning dynamic tasks~\cite{jakobsson2026wiggle}.
Sampling-based planning has also been combined with repeated
fitting of learned dynamics~\cite{williams2017information}.

We present \emph{AeroWhip}, a framework that combines command-to-tip
prediction, offline trajectory optimization, and model refinement
between flight batches. The predictor connects a fitted loaded-vehicle
response, the motion of an offset attachment, and a reduced
differentiable rod model with learned corrections. An MPPI-inspired
optimizer~\cite{williams2017model} uses this predictor to balance
whipping-shape agreement, directed tip motion, and target approach
under vehicle-motion constraints. Measurements from physical
executions then refine the predictor used for subsequent planning.
These components form the sim-to-real-to-sim procedure evaluated
in this study. We examine its effects on physical target approach
and command-to-tip prediction accuracy.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem_statement}

We consider a quadrotor carrying a cable of length $L>0$, with
one end attached to the vehicle and the other end free. The
attachment point is fixed relative to the quadrotor, and the cable
is modeled as free to pivot at that point. Planning begins from
nominal hover with the cable hanging at rest. The target is
stationary. Vectors are written in bold lowercase and matrices in
bold uppercase. Unless otherwise stated, Cartesian vectors are expressed
in the world frame $\{W\}$. The study considers one fixed target
and a forward strike direction chosen as world $+x$; generalization
to other target directions is outside its scope.

The whipping motion considered here uses attachment motion to
create a fold that travels toward the free end, as illustrated
in Fig.~\ref{fig:problem_whipping}. The cable's inertia allows
parts of it to lag behind the attachment, while its flexibility
allows different portions to bend and change direction.
A travelling fold is a moving feature of the cable's shape:
successive portions of the cable bend and straighten as the
fold passes through them. Near the free end, the fold can
unfold as the tip extends outward~\cite{MCMILLEN2003192}.
The desired motion consists of fold formation, propagation,
and extension toward the target. Both the geometry and timing
of this sequence matter; related simulations demonstrate
target-directed whip motions whose target approach is sensitive
to movement duration~\cite{nah2023learning}.

Let $t\geq0$ denote elapsed time from the start of the maneuver.
Each point along the cable has a fixed material label
$s\in[0,1]$. The point labeled $s$ lies a distance $sL$ from
the attachment, measured along the cable in its unstretched
configuration. Thus, $s=0$ identifies the attachment, $s=0.5$
identifies the point halfway along the cable, and $s=1$
identifies the free tip. Each label follows the same cable
point throughout the maneuver, as shown by the blue midpoint
marker in Fig.~\ref{fig:problem_whipping}.

The vector $\mathbf{c}(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ gives the
world-frame position of the point labeled $s$ at time $t$.
At a fixed time, varying $s$ from $0$ to $1$ traces the cable's
centerline. At a fixed $s$, varying $t$ follows the motion
of that particular cable point. For example,
$\mathbf{c}(0.5,t_2)$ gives the midpoint position at time $t_2$.
The attachment and tip positions are $\mathbf{c}(0,t)$ and
$\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{tip}}(t)=\mathbf{c}(1,t)$, respectively.
The tip velocity is
$\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{tip}}(t)=\partial_t\mathbf{c}(1,t)$.

Cable deformation relative to the moving attachment is described
by the normalized shape
\begin{equation}
\bar{\mathbf{c}}(s,t)
=
\frac{\mathbf{c}(s,t)-\mathbf{c}(0,t)}{L}.
\label{eq:normalized_whip_shape}
\end{equation}
This removes attachment translation and normalizes distances by
cable length, while retaining the shape's orientation in the
world frame.

Let $M\in\mathbb{N}$ be the number of shape samples, with
$M\geq3$ so that interior bending can be represented.
The samples have fixed material labels $s_j=j/(M-1)$,
for $j=0,\ldots,M-1$, equally spaced along the unstretched
cable. They are interpolated centerline samples, rather than
physical measurement markers, and the same labels are used
at every time.
% \edward{(resolve) define M better, number of markers in the cable, include M can only be $\mathbb{N}$}
The unit direction between neighboring samples is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{d}_j(t)
=
\frac{
\bar{\mathbf{c}}(s_{j+1},t)-\bar{\mathbf{c}}(s_j,t)
}{
\left\|
\bar{\mathbf{c}}(s_{j+1},t)-\bar{\mathbf{c}}(s_j,t)
\right\|_2
},
\label{eq:whip_segment_direction}
\end{equation}
for $j=0,\ldots,M-2$ and nonzero sample separation.
The sampled positions describe the overall cable shape, while
the segment directions describe local orientation. Differences
between neighboring directions indicate local bending.
A fold travelling toward the tip appears as a region of
pronounced direction change that moves toward increasing $s$.

Let $K\in\mathbb{N}$ be the number of time samples, with
$K\geq2$, at ordered times $t_0<\cdots<t_{K-1}$.
% \edward{(resolve) same as M definition, what is K? specific for time?, also $\mathbb{N}$?}
At each time $t_k$, let
$\mathbf{C}_k\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times M}$ collect the sampled normalized
positions $\bar{\mathbf{c}}(s_j,t_k)$ as columns, and let
$\mathbf{D}_k\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times(M-1)}$ collect the segment directions
$\mathbf{d}_j(t_k)$ as columns.
Here $j$ indexes cable points and $k$ indexes time samples.
The ordered shape history is
$\mathcal{W}=((\mathbf{C}_k,\mathbf{D}_k))_{k=0}^{K-1}$.
Its sampling times are specified separately; the history records
the order of the configurations, without fixing their physical
duration. The offline planner compares candidate and reference
histories at normalized maneuver phases. Tip position and velocity
describe the resulting target approach in physical space and time.

\subsection{DDER Cable Model}
\label{subsec:dder_model}

We adopt a reduced discrete elastic rod
representation~\cite{bergou2008discrete}, implemented in
differentiable form following the approach of combining rod
mechanics and learning in DEFORM~\cite{pmlr-v270-chen25d}.
The centerline consists of $N\in\mathbb{N}$ simulation nodes,
with $N\geq3$. Their positions and velocities are
$\mathbf{r}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ and
$\mathbf{v}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$, for $i=0,\ldots,N-1$.
Let $\mathbf{r}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ stack these positions and
velocities. The cable state is
$\mathbf{x}_c=[\mathbf{r}^{\top},\mathbf{v}^{\top}]^{\top}
\in\mathbb{R}^{6N}$.
Node $0$ follows the attachment position $\mathbf{p}_a(t)$,
and node $N-1$ is the free tip:
$\mathbf{r}_0=\mathbf{p}_a$ and
$\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{tip}}=\mathbf{r}_{N-1}$.

The model assumes uniform material properties and neglects axial extension.
Neighboring nodes therefore satisfy
$\|\mathbf{r}_{i+1}-\mathbf{r}_i\|_2=\ell_i$,
where $\ell_i$ is the fixed segment length and
$\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\ell_i=L$.
The simulated centerline $\mathbf{c}(s,t)$ is obtained by
interpolating the node positions at their cumulative rest-length
coordinates. Thus, $N$ counts simulation nodes, $M$ counts
interpolated shape samples, and $K$ counts samples in time.
These counts have distinct roles and need not be equal.

We assume a straight undeformed shape and isotropic bending,
meaning equal resistance to bending in different directions.
Material twist describes rotation of the cable cross-sections
about the centerline.
The attachment position is prescribed without imposing a
cross-section orientation or twisting moment at either end.
Transient rotation of the cross-sections is not simulated;
instead, their orientations are assumed to relax to elastic
equilibrium at each instant. This is the quasistatic
material-frame approximation. For a naturally straight,
isotropic rod with these rotational boundary conditions,
the equilibrium material twist is zero~\cite{bergou2008discrete}.
Independent twist coordinates and torsional energy are therefore
omitted. The centerline still bends in three dimensions;
the reduction does not impose planar motion.

Bending depends on changes in direction between neighboring
segments. Let $\mathbf{t}_i$ be the unit vector along segment
$i$ and $\bar{\ell}_i=(\ell_{i-1}+\ell_i)/2$.
Following the discrete curvature and isotropic bending energy
of Bergou et al.~\cite{bergou2008discrete}, the model uses
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_i^b
&=
\frac{2\,\mathbf{t}_{i-1}\times\mathbf{t}_i}
{1+\mathbf{t}_{i-1}^{\top}\mathbf{t}_i},\\
E_b(\mathbf{r})
&=
\frac{EI}{2}
\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}
\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}_i^b\|_2^2}{\bar{\ell}_i},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:bending_energy}
\end{equation}
where $EI>0$ is the bending stiffness and
$\boldsymbol{\kappa}_i^b$ is the dimensionless discrete curvature binormal.
Its magnitude measures the change in direction between adjacent
segments. The bending force is
$\mathbf{f}_b=-\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}E_b$.
The curvature expression assumes neighboring tangents are not
exactly antiparallel. Numerical force evaluation uses a small
positive denominator floor near this singular configuration.

\subsection*{Aerial Cable Whipping Task}
The task combines a desired cable-shape sequence with target
approach, subject to limits on desired and predicted vehicle motion.

Let $\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{tar}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ denote the target,
$[0,T]$ the targeting interval, and $\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ref}}$ a
reference whipping motion expressed using the representation
in Section~\ref{subsec:whipping_motion}.
The superscript $\mathrm{ref}$ identifies fixed reference quantities.
The reference specifies the desired evolution of the cable shape.
It remains fixed while the prediction models are refined.
Target approach is measured by the minimum
three-dimensional tip-to-target distance:
\begin{equation}
e_{\mathrm{tar}}
=
\min_{t\in[0,T]}
\left\|
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{tip}}(t)
-\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{tar}}
\right\|_2.
\label{eq:target_distance}
\end{equation}
For measured trajectories, the minimum is evaluated along
straight segments between adjacent valid samples within this
interval. Tracking gaps are not bridged and are reported
separately. This metric quantifies target approach; the shape
history and tip velocity describe the associated whipping motion.

A desired trajectory is admissible for planning if it satisfies
the prescribed desired-trajectory bounds and the simulation
satisfies the vehicle-altitude, cable ground-clearance, and
numerical validity checks. The command bounds constrain speed,
jerk, altitude, and the magnitude and direction of
$\mathbf{a}_d+g\mathbf{e}_3$, where $\mathbf{a}_d$ is the desired
kinematic acceleration, $g$ is gravitational acceleration, and
$\mathbf{e}_3$ is the upward world unit vector.
These planning limits are specified with the optimization procedure.

\noindent\textbf{Problem (Aerial Cable Whipping).}
Given an initial quadrotor--cable configuration, a target, and a
reference whipping motion, develop a prediction and planning
framework that generates admissible quadrotor trajectories
to bring the physical cable tip close to the target through
the desired whipping motion. Use measurements from physical
executions to refine the quadrotor and cable models for
subsequent prediction and planning.

The framework addresses three connected subproblems.

\noindent\textbf{Subproblem 1 (Command-to-Tip Prediction).}
Construct a model that predicts cable motion from desired
quadrotor commands by accounting for the loaded vehicle's
response, attachment geometry, and cable dynamics.

\noindent\textbf{Subproblem 2 (Offline Trajectory Optimization).}
Given the current prediction model, nominal initial condition,
target, and targeting interval, determine an admissible
quadrotor command sequence that balances agreement with
$\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ref}}$ and predicted target approach.

\noindent\textbf{Subproblem 3 (Model Refinement).}
Given the executed commands, measured quadrotor motion, and
measured cable positions, update the quadrotor-response and
cable models. Use the updated predictor to plan subsequent
maneuvers, completing the sim-to-real-to-sim cycle.

\section{AeroWhip Framework}
\label{sec:aerowhip_framework}

AeroWhip connects a fitted vehicle response to cable dynamics
through the attachment geometry. This predictor supplies the
trajectory optimizer with simulated vehicle and cable motion;
recorded executions provide the data for model refinement.
Planning is offline: the flight controller tracks the fixed desired
trajectory, with no cable-state replanning during the maneuver.

\subsection{Quadrotor and Cable Prediction Model}
\label{subsec:prediction_model}

The predictor maps desired quadrotor commands to cable motion
in two steps. It first predicts the motion of the quadrotor
carrying the cable. The predicted vehicle pose then determines
the attachment trajectory that drives the cable model.
The fitted quadrotor response approximates cable-loading
effects over the motion conditions represented in its training
data. Instantaneous cable configuration and reaction forces
are not explicit inputs to the vehicle prediction.

\subsubsection{Quadrotor response}
The input consists of desired position $\mathbf{p}_d$, velocity
$\mathbf{v}_d$, acceleration $\mathbf{a}_d$, and yaw $\psi_d$,
held between command updates. The subscript $d$ denotes a desired
quantity. Let $\mathbf{p}_q$ and $\mathbf{v}_q$ denote the
position and velocity of the quadrotor's tracked origin.
The rotation matrix $\mathbf{R}\in \mathrm{SO}(3)$ maps vectors from the
body-fixed tracking frame into the world frame.

We model the loaded vehicle's translational response using
position and velocity tracking errors, acceleration feedforward,
and a constant acceleration bias:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{nom}}(t)
={}&\mathbf{K}_p[\mathbf{p}_d(t-\tau_d)-\mathbf{p}_q(t)]\\
&+\mathbf{K}_v[\mathbf{v}_d(t-\tau_d)-\mathbf{v}_q(t)]\\
&+\mathbf{K}_a\mathbf{a}_d(t-\tau_d)+\mathbf{b}_q.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:quadrotor_response}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{nom}}$ is the nominal acceleration,
$\tau_d\geq0$ is an effective response delay, and
$\mathbf{K}_p,\mathbf{K}_v,\mathbf{K}_a$ are fitted diagonal gain matrices.
Each matrix uses a shared horizontal coefficient and a separate
vertical coefficient. The bias $\mathbf{b}_q$ remains constant
during a prediction. The predicted motion satisfies
$\dot{\mathbf{p}}_q=\mathbf{v}_q$ and
$\dot{\mathbf{v}}_q=\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{nom}}
+\Delta\mathbf{a}_q$, where $\Delta\mathbf{a}_q$ is the learned
acceleration correction described below.

Attitude affects the motion of the offset cable attachment.
To construct the desired orientation, let $\mathbf{R}_c$ describe a
command-attitude frame in world coordinates. Its third axis lies
along $\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{nom}}+g\mathbf{e}_3$, where $\mathbf{S}$ is a
positive diagonal matrix of fitted acceleration scales, with a
shared horizontal value and a separate vertical value. Its first
axis is the normalized projection of the horizontal heading
specified by $\psi_d(t-\tau_d)$ onto the plane perpendicular
to the third axis; the second completes a right-handed frame.
Let $\mathbf{Q}\in \mathrm{SO}(3)$ map tracking-frame vectors into this
command-attitude frame. The desired orientation in the convention
of $\mathbf{R}$ is then $\mathbf{R}_d=\mathbf{R}_c \mathbf{Q}$. The alignment $\mathbf{Q}$ remains fixed
during each prediction.

Let $\mathbf{e}_R$ be the principal rotation vector of
$\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R}_d$, expressed in the tracking frame. Its direction
gives the rotation axis and its magnitude gives the rotation
angle in radians. The second-order attitude response is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\dot{\mathbf{R}}&=\mathbf{R}[\boldsymbol\omega]_\times,\\
\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}
&=\frac{\mathbf{e}_R}{\tau_R^2}
-\frac{2\boldsymbol\omega}{\tau_R},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:attitude_response}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol\omega$ is angular velocity in the tracking
frame, $\tau_R>0$ is the fitted attitude-response timescale,
and $[\boldsymbol\omega]_\times$ is the matrix representing
cross product with $\boldsymbol\omega$.
The first equation updates orientation from angular velocity;
the second drives the orientation toward $\mathbf{R}_d$ while damping
angular motion. The desired orientation uses the nominal
acceleration; the learned correction enters the translational
response. The correction represents additional realized
acceleration, rather than an inferred thrust command; it still
affects later attitude predictions through the evolving position
and velocity tracking errors.

We fit the nominal translation parameters to measured position
trajectories and separately fit the attitude scales $\mathbf{S}$ and
response time $\tau_R$ to rotation-vector errors between predicted
and measured orientations.
Offline planning starts with $\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$, $\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{I}$,
zero vehicle and cable velocities, zero angular velocity,
and $\mathbf b_q=\mathbf0$.
The cable initially hangs straight. Commands before the maneuver
hold the initial desired position with zero desired velocity and
acceleration. Recorded-flight fitting and prediction instead use
pre-maneuver measurements to initialize the state, bias, and
effective alignment. Predictions initialized this way are computed
for fitting or postflight model comparison. They are distinguished
from the original forecasts saved before execution, which retain
the nominal planning initialization.

\subsubsection{Attachment motion and cable dynamics}
Let $\boldsymbol\rho_a$ be the fixed offset from the tracked
origin to the cable attachment, expressed in the tracking frame.
The predicted attachment position is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_a=\mathbf{p}_q+\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol\rho_a.
\label{eq:attachment_mapping}
\end{equation}
Both translation and rotation contribute to the prescribed
attachment position $\mathbf r_0=\mathbf p_a$ in the DDER model
introduced in Section~\ref{subsec:dder_model}.
The cable solver linearly interpolates successive attachment
positions and obtains the boundary velocity from their difference
over the integration interval.

The remaining nodes move under bending, damping, gravity,
length constraints, and learned acceleration corrections:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
m_i\dot{\mathbf{v}}_i
&=\mathbf{f}_{b,i}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{damp},i}
  +\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{con},i}\\
&\quad-m_i g\mathbf{e}_3+m_i\Delta\mathbf{a}_{c,i},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:driven_cable_dynamics}
\end{equation}
for $i=1,\ldots,N-1$, with
$\dot{\mathbf{r}}_i=\mathbf{v}_i$.
Here $m_i$ is the node mass, $\mathbf{f}_{b,i}$ is the bending
force obtained from~\eqref{eq:bending_energy}, and
$\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{con},i}$ denotes the implicit reaction
required to preserve the segment lengths. Numerically, mass-weighted
position and velocity projections implement this reaction after
the unconstrained dynamics update.
The damping force combines linear drag
$-m_i\gamma_c\mathbf v_i$, with $\gamma_c\geq0$, and bending
damping with coefficient $C_b\geq0$.

Bending damping uses a regularized curvature-binormal rate
$\dot{\boldsymbol\kappa}_{i,\mathrm{rel}}^b$ to resist changes in
bending while reducing sensitivity to local rigid rotation.
At an interior vertex $i$, write $\mathbf a=\mathbf t_{i-1}$,
$\mathbf b=\mathbf t_i$, $\mathbf n=\mathbf a\times\mathbf b$,
$c=\mathbf a^\top\mathbf b$, and $d=1+c$.
The vertex index is suppressed on these local quantities.
Dots denote time derivatives computed from the node velocities;
in particular, $\dot{\boldsymbol\kappa}_i^b$ is the derivative of
the curvature expression in~\eqref{eq:bending_energy}.
Let $\varepsilon=2\times10^{-5}$ be a fixed dimensionless
regularization parameter and
$\alpha=\varepsilon/(\|\mathbf n\|_2^2+\varepsilon)$.
The local angular-velocity estimate and regularized rate are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol\omega_s
&=\tfrac12(\mathbf I-\mathbf a\mathbf a^\top)
(\mathbf a\times\dot{\mathbf a}+\mathbf b\times\dot{\mathbf b}),\\
\dot{\boldsymbol\kappa}_{i,\mathrm{rel}}^b
&=-\frac{2\mathbf n\dot c}{d(\|\mathbf n\|_2^2+\varepsilon)}\\
&\quad+\alpha\bigl(\dot{\boldsymbol\kappa}_i^b
-\boldsymbol\omega_s\times\boldsymbol\kappa_i^b\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:regularized_bending_rate}
\end{equation}
The weight $\alpha$ blends a corotational rate for bent segments
with a finite expression near a straight configuration.
At $\mathbf a=\mathbf b=\mathbf t$, the rate reduces to
$\mathbf t\times(\dot{\mathbf b}-\dot{\mathbf a})$,
so no bending plane needs to be selected.
For positive $\varepsilon$, removal of rigid-rotation effects
is approximate.
Its quadratic dissipation and the resulting stacked damping force are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal D_b
&=\frac{C_b}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}
\frac{\|\dot{\boldsymbol\kappa}_{i,\mathrm{rel}}^b\|_2^2}
{\bar\ell_i},\\
\mathbf f_{\mathrm{bd}}&=-\nabla_{\mathbf v}\mathcal D_b.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:bending_damping}
\end{equation}
During each integration step, elastic, gravitational, and learned accelerations update
velocity, drag is applied, and bending damping is solved
implicitly. After advancing the positions, position and velocity
projections enforce the segment lengths and their velocity constraints.

\subsubsection{Learned acceleration corrections}
At each simulation step, neural networks supply state-dependent
acceleration corrections to represent discrepancies between
predicted and observed motion. Integrated with the nominal
dynamics, these corrections affect subsequent predicted motion.

The quadrotor network outputs one three-dimensional correction
$\Delta\mathbf{a}_q$, giving
$\dot{\mathbf{v}}_q=\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{nom}}
+\Delta\mathbf{a}_q$.
Its inputs are the position and velocity tracking errors
in~\eqref{eq:quadrotor_response}, delayed desired acceleration,
predicted velocity, and the bias $\mathbf{b}_q$.

A single cable network takes the relative positions
$\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_0$ and velocities $\mathbf v_i-\mathbf v_0$
of all cable nodes, together with $\mathbf v_0$, the boundary
velocity carried by the cable state. It jointly outputs a
three-dimensional correction $\Delta\mathbf{a}_{c,i}$ for each free node
$i=1,\ldots,N-1$. Thus, each node's correction can depend on
the configuration and motion of the whole cable.
The correction contributes the force
$m_i\Delta\mathbf{a}_{c,i}$
in~\eqref{eq:driven_cable_dynamics}, alongside bending,
damping, gravity, and length constraints.
This use of learned corrections alongside rod mechanics
is motivated by DEFORM~\cite{pmlr-v270-chen25d}.

Both networks are multilayer perceptrons. All vector inputs
and outputs use world-frame components, with fixed scaling
applied to the inputs. A scaled hyperbolic tangent bounds
each output acceleration component.
A smooth motion-dependent factor multiplies the quadrotor
correction. It is zero when predicted velocity, delayed desired
velocity, and delayed desired acceleration are all zero,
and increases toward one as their magnitudes increase.
The cable correction is zero at the attachment,
preserving its prescribed motion.

We fit the network weights by minimizing differences between
predicted and recorded position trajectories. The quadrotor fit
uses recorded commands and vehicle positions; the cable fit uses
measured cable positions
and is driven by the measured attachment trajectory.
Gradients are propagated through the simulated motion to
update the network weights, so measured acceleration labels
are not required.
Cable fitting is therefore conditional on measured attachment
motion. Planning and command-to-tip evaluation instead use the
predicted attachment motion, so their cable-tip errors include
the upstream vehicle-prediction error. After fitting, network
weights remain fixed during offline planning, while corrections
are recomputed as the predicted state evolves.

\subsection{Offline Trajectory Optimization}
\label{subsec:offline_planning}

The offline optimizer searches for a quadrotor command sequence
that produces the desired whipping motion toward the target.
Each candidate is evaluated with the current prediction model,
whose parameters remain fixed throughout the search.

\subsubsection{Command representation and constraints}
We search over editable jerk sequences derived from two fixed
templates. One is the command sequence associated with the reference
whip described below; the other is a trajectory found by an earlier
search using the retained baseline model. These templates supply
motion priors. Their predicted vehicle and cable trajectories are
recomputed with the current model whenever they are evaluated.

Each template is divided into three ordered time intervals with
boundaries at $0$, $0.3T$, $0.6T$, and $T$. These are command-time
landmarks, not detected phases of cable motion. For each axis,
two timing parameters set three positive duration fractions through
a softmax mapping, preserving the total duration $T$. One strength
parameter scales the retimed template, and ten equally spaced
support values provide an additive correction. The search vector
$\boldsymbol\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{39}$ therefore contains
$(2+1+10)\times3$ parameters.

Retiming uses interval averages of the piecewise-constant template
jerk. The strength and linearly interpolated corrections act in
an unconstrained representation, followed by a hyperbolic-tangent
mapping that bounds each jerk component. Each resulting jerk
value is held over one command interval and integrated exactly
to obtain desired position, velocity, and acceleration.
The PVA commands are sampled at 30~Hz over $[0,T]$ and held
between updates, with desired yaw fixed at zero.

Command checks apply to the sampled PVA packets: they bound
speed, height, and the magnitude, tilt, and vertical component
of $\mathbf a_d+g\mathbf e_3$. These restrict desired motion;
they do not measure actual thrust or attitude limits.
Separate checks apply to predicted vehicle height, cable floor
clearance, and finite, bounded simulation states. Violating
a check rejects the candidate.

\subsubsection{Trajectory objective}
The objective rewards a whipping-shape sequence that brings the
tip toward the target with forward velocity and cable extension.
It also penalizes excessive vehicle motion and a difficult
transition to recovery. The score to maximize is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
J(\boldsymbol\theta)
={}&w_{\mathrm{whip}}P S_{\mathrm{whip}}
   +w_{\mathrm{enc}}Q_{\mathrm{enc}}\\
&+w_{\mathrm{ext}}Q_{\mathrm{ext}}
   -w_{\mathrm{miss}}(1-P)\\
&-C_{\mathrm{motion}}-C_{\mathrm{exit}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:planning_score}
\end{equation}
Here $P$ measures target proximity, $S_{\mathrm{whip}}$ measures
reference-shape agreement, and $Q_{\mathrm{enc}}$ and
$Q_{\mathrm{ext}}$ reward directed tip motion. The four weights
are positive. All terms are evaluated from the trajectory predicted
for $\boldsymbol\theta$. We define them below using the prescribed
strike direction $\mathbf e=(1,0,0)^\top$.

\noindent\emph{Target approach and directed motion.}
A sphere of radius $r_{\mathrm{tar}}$ centered at
$\mathbf p_{\mathrm{tar}}$ defines the planning tolerance region.
If any cable node enters it, the encounter time $t_e$ is the
first entry time; otherwise, $t_e$ is the time of closest tip
approach. Entry is detected along each node's linearly interpolated
path between simulation states. This is a node-based geometric
approximation: it does not test the cable segments between nodes
or simulate collision forces. Let
$d_e=\|\mathbf r_{\mathrm{tip}}(t_e)-\mathbf p_{\mathrm{tar}}\|_2$.
The proximity score $P=\exp[-(d_e/\sigma_p)^2]$, with
$\sigma_p>0$, increases as the tip approaches the target.
Its encounter-based distance is distinct from the minimum-distance
evaluation in~\eqref{eq:target_distance}.

At $t_e$, let $v_\parallel=\mathbf e^\top\mathbf v_{\mathrm{tip}}$
and $v_{q,\parallel}=\mathbf e^\top\mathbf v_q$ be the forward
tip and vehicle velocities. Let $b_e$ be the vehicle's retraction
from its greatest previous forward position, and let
$\ell_e=\mathbf e^\top(\mathbf r_{\mathrm{tip}}-\mathbf p_a)$
be the forward attachment-to-tip extension.
Define $\operatorname{clip}(z)=\min(1,\max(0,z))$ and
$h(z)=0.2+0.8\operatorname{clip}(z)$. The two motion scores are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
Q_{\mathrm{enc}}
&=P\chi\,h(v_\parallel/v_s)
  h(-v_{q,\parallel}/v_{q,s})h(b_e/b_s),\\
Q_{\mathrm{ext}}
&=P\chi\,\operatorname{clip}(v_\parallel/v_s)
  \operatorname{clip}(\ell_e/L)\\
&\qquad\times
  \operatorname{clip}\!\left(
  \frac{v_\parallel}{\|\mathbf v_{\mathrm{tip}}\|_2}\right)^2.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:directed_motion_scores}
\end{equation}
The positive scales $v_s$, $v_{q,s}$, and $b_s$ set saturation
levels for tip speed, vehicle reversal, and retraction.
The alignment factor in $Q_{\mathrm{ext}}$ is zero at zero
tip speed. The indicator $\chi$ is one when no node enters
the sphere or when the tip enters strictly before every other
node, and zero otherwise. An earlier interior-node entry fixes
$t_e$ before any later tip approach. Thus, the scores favor
tip-first approach according to the node-based test.
The nonzero floor in $h$ gives partial approach credit before
all three motion features develop; reversal is encouraged,
rather than required.

\noindent\emph{Whipping-shape agreement.}
We selected $\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{ref}}$ from an earlier simulated maneuver
for its fold formation, propagation, and extension. That maneuver
was generated by an earlier MPPI planner using a different
development model, with the same target position relative to
the launch. Its normalized cable-shape history supplies the
scoring reference; its jerk commands also supply one of the
two search templates. The method therefore uses prior motion
information in both the objective and the search representation.
The reference and templates were frozen before the retained
baseline plan and remain fixed across model updates.

Let $t_{\mathrm{ref}}>0$ be the reference motion's first target
encounter time. Its samples occur at
$t_k^{\mathrm{ref}}=t_{\mathrm{ref}}k/(K-1)$.
The matrices $\mathbf{C}_k^{\mathrm{ref}}$ and
$\mathbf{D}_k^{\mathrm{ref}}$ contain the reference's normalized
positions and segment directions at these times.
Saved positions are interpolated in time and resampled at the
fixed material coordinates of Section~\ref{subsec:whipping_motion}.
The planner uses $M=21$ shape samples and $K=21$ time samples.
The normalized shape removes attachment translation and scales
distances by cable length, while retaining world-frame orientation.

Candidate and reference histories are compared at normalized
maneuver phases. For $t_e>0$, candidate sampling times are
$t_k(\gamma)=t_e[k/(K-1)]^\gamma$, with
$\gamma\in\Gamma=\{3/4,1,4/3\}$.
Every choice preserves order and covers the full interval $[0,t_e]$;
$\gamma=1$ gives uniform phase sampling. Let $\mathbf{C}_k(\gamma)$
and $\mathbf{D}_k(\gamma)$ collect the normalized positions and unit
segment directions at these times. Their agreement with the
reference is measured by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
E_C(\gamma)
&=\frac{1}{KM\sigma_C^2}
\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}
\|\mathbf{C}_k(\gamma)-\mathbf{C}_k^{\mathrm{ref}}\|_F^2,\\
E_D(\gamma)
&=\frac{1}{K(M-1)\sigma_D^2}
\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}
\|\mathbf{D}_k(\gamma)-\mathbf{D}_k^{\mathrm{ref}}\|_F^2,\\
S_{\mathrm{whip}}
&=\max_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
e^{-E_C(\gamma)-E_D(\gamma)-\beta|\log\gamma|}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:whip_similarity}
\end{equation}
Here $\|\cdot\|_F$ is the Frobenius norm,
$\sigma_C,\sigma_D>0$ set the comparison scales, and
$\beta\geq0$ penalizes departure from uniform phase sampling.
$E_C$ compares overall shape positions and $E_D$ compares
local segment orientations. If $t_e=0$, we set $S_{\mathrm{whip}}=0$.
This score encourages the reference's
fold sequence without explicitly detecting or tracking a fold.
Overall duration differences are normalized out; directed-speed
rewards, the horizon, and command bounds govern physical timing.

\noindent\emph{Motion and recovery costs.}
Let $x_q(t)$ and $y_q(t)$ be the predicted vehicle displacements
from its initial position along $\mathbf e$ and the perpendicular
horizontal direction $\mathbf e_3\times\mathbf e$, respectively.
Let $\bar{\mathbf j}_n$ be the jerk in command interval $n$,
normalized componentwise by its bounds.
Scoring ends at the first tip entry or $T$, whichever comes first;
denote this time by $t_f$. Let $\Delta t=1/30$~s be the command
period and $t_n=n\Delta t$, for $n=0,1,\ldots$, the command times.
With $\Delta t_n=\max(0,\min(\Delta t,t_f-t_n))$,
the motion cost is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
C_{\mathrm{motion}}
&=\sum_{n:t_n<T}\Delta t_n\Bigl[\alpha_f[x_q(t_n)]_+^2\\
&\qquad+\alpha_l y_q(t_n)^2
  +\frac{\alpha_j}{3}\|\bar{\mathbf j}_n\|_2^2\Bigr],
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:motion_cost}
\end{equation}
where $[z]_+=\max(0,z)$ and the $\alpha$ weights are positive.
This time-weighted penalty discourages excessive forward and
lateral vehicle travel and jerk, while permitting the motion
needed to initiate whipping.

The optimized command continues to the boundary ending the
30~Hz interval containing $t_f$, where recovery begins.
If no tip entry occurs, this handover is at $T$.
Using desired velocity and acceleration at the handover,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
C_{\mathrm{exit}}
={}&\eta_v\|\mathbf v_d\|_2^2
  +\eta_z[\mathbf e_3^\top\mathbf v_d]_+^2\\
&+\eta_a\left[
  \frac{\mathbf v_d^\top\mathbf a_d}{\|\mathbf v_d\|_2}
  \right]_+^2,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:exit_cost}
\end{equation}
with positive weights $\eta_v,\eta_z,\eta_a$ and the final
term defined as zero at zero desired speed. These penalties
discourage entering recovery with high speed, upward motion,
or continued acceleration along the direction of travel.
An interior-node encounter can precede $t_f$. Physical target
distance is evaluated over the fixed interval $[0,T]$, which
can include early recovery. Infeasible candidates receive
$J=-\infty$.

\subsubsection{Sampling-based optimization}
An offline MPPI-inspired search maximizes~\eqref{eq:planning_score}
using exponential trajectory weighting~\cite{williams2017model}.
The search maintains several proposal means to explore different
motions. For each mean $\boldsymbol\mu$, we sample
$\boldsymbol\theta^{(b)}=\boldsymbol\mu+
\boldsymbol\epsilon^{(b)}$, where the perturbations follow
a fixed mixture of diagonal Gaussian distributions.
The candidates are simulated in parallel.
For this proposal, let $\mathcal V$ contain the indices of feasible
random samples and $J_b=J(\boldsymbol\theta^{(b)})$ denote their scores.
For a proposal with feasible samples, define weights $w_b$,
$b\in\mathcal V$, and update the mean as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
w_b&=\frac{\exp(J_b/\lambda)}
{\sum_{a\in\mathcal V}\exp(J_a/\lambda)},\\
\boldsymbol\mu^{+}
&=\sum_{b\in\mathcal V}w_b\boldsymbol\theta^{(b)}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:offline_sampling_update}
\end{equation}
Proposals without feasible samples retain their means.
Here $\lambda>0$ controls how strongly higher scores are
favored. Its adjustment targets a prescribed effective sample
count, $(\sum_{b\in\mathcal V}w_b^2)^{-1}$.
Proposal means, fixed templates, and the retained best command
are also evaluated, but only random samples enter the weighted
update. We retain the highest-scoring feasible candidate across
iterations and stop when improvement plateaus under a fixed
tolerance and patience rule.

We simulate the selected command individually to verify its
score and feasibility. Quintic return segments are then
appended to the optimized prefix, matching its handover PVA
and ending at the launch position with zero desired velocity
and acceleration, followed by a stationary hold.
Return durations are selected subject to the command bounds.
The complete sequence is replayed to check the preserved prefix,
return, and predicted vehicle and cable motion.
If recovery construction or verification fails, no command
sequence is exported. Otherwise, the fixed sequence is exported
for execution by the vehicle's existing tracking controller.



\subsection{Model Refinement from Physical Executions}
\label{subsec:model_refinement}

We refine the prediction model between batches of physical executions.
Let $\mathcal M_r$ denote the predictor used in refinement round $r$.
The offline planner uses $\mathcal M_r$ to produce a command sequence;
repeated executions supply data for fitting $\mathcal M_{r+1}$.
The initial predictor
$\mathcal M_0$ retains the calibration obtained from preliminary
motions. Each update starts from the preceding model and refines
its response parameters, cable coefficients, and learned corrections.
Cable length, masses, attachment geometry, and numerical integration
settings remain fixed.

\subsubsection{Recorded execution data}
Each recording contains the executed command schedule, measured
quadrotor position and orientation, and cable-marker positions.
The measured attachment trajectory is computed
using~\eqref{eq:attachment_mapping} with the measured vehicle pose.
Each fitting window is initialized from preceding measurements,
then simulated recursively without resetting predicted states to
later observations. Missing observations receive zero loss weight.

The fitting set combines designated recordings from the new batch,
earlier whipping batches, and preliminary motions. These three
groups receive relative weights $1$, $0.5$, and $0.5$, normalized
over the groups present. Recordings have equal weight within each
group, shared among their eligible fitting windows. This retains
earlier motion conditions while emphasizing the latest executions.
Validation recordings are separated before fitting; final test
recordings remain outside fitting and tuning.

\subsubsection{Staged model fitting}
Each stage minimizes a weighted simulation-error loss of the form
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data}}
&=\sum_{o\in\mathcal O}w_o\,
\rho\!\left(\frac{\|\mathbf e_o\|_2^2}{\sigma_o^2}\right),\\
\rho(z)&=2\bigl(\sqrt{1+z}-1\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:refinement_data_loss}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal O$ contains the valid observations for that stage,
and $\mathbf e_o\in\mathbb{R}^3$ is the prediction error.
The weights satisfy $w_o\geq0$ and $\sum_{o\in\mathcal O}w_o=1$.
Position errors are differences between simulated and measured
positions. Orientation errors are the principal rotation vectors
of $\mathbf{R}_o^\top \mathbf{R}_o^{\mathrm{meas}}$, where $\mathbf{R}_o$ and
$\mathbf{R}_o^{\mathrm{meas}}$ are the predicted and measured orientations
at observation $o$, respectively.
The scales $\sigma_o$ are $0.02$~m for position and $0.05$~rad
for orientation. They set the loss sensitivity; the robust penalty
reduces the influence of large errors. Cable fitting assigns half
the observation weight to all non-attachment markers, including
the tip, and half to the tip alone.

The update proceeds in four stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Quadrotor response.}
    Fit the six horizontal and vertical coefficients in
    $\mathbf{K}_p,\mathbf{K}_v,\mathbf{K}_a$ to measured position trajectories while keeping
    the inherited quadrotor correction fixed. The effective delay
    $\tau_d$ is selected from a predefined candidate set using
    regularized training loss. Fit the two scales in $\mathbf{S}$ and
    the response time $\tau_R$ separately to orientation errors.

    \item \emph{Quadrotor correction.}
    Hold the nominal translation parameters fixed and update the
    quadrotor network through the simulated position trajectories.
    Refit $\mathbf{S}$ and $\tau_R$ afterward, since the corrected
    translation changes the acceleration history that drives
    the attitude response.

    \item \emph{Cable physics.}
    Fit $EI$, $C_b$, and $\gamma_c$ with the inherited cable
    correction fixed. The cable is driven by the measured
    attachment trajectory, so this fit conditions on observed
    boundary motion rather than predicted quadrotor motion.

    \item \emph{Cable correction.}
    Hold the fitted cable coefficients fixed and update the cable
    network through the simulated marker trajectories. The same
    measured attachment trajectory and marker loss are used.
\end{enumerate}

Positive nominal parameters are fitted in logarithmic coordinates
with fixed bounds. A quadratic penalty on their change in log
coordinates limits departure from the values at the start of each
fit; a separate quadratic penalty regularizes the delay change.
Both penalties have coefficient $0.03$, with the delay change
normalized by $0.04$~s before squaring. These stages use bounded
nonlinear least squares.

Both neural stages penalize correction magnitude and changes from
the inherited network. Their objective is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{NN}}
={}&\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data}}\\
&+\frac{\lambda_a}{3a_{\max}^2}
\Bigl\langle\|\Delta\mathbf a\|_2^2
+\|\Delta\mathbf a
 -\Delta\mathbf a^{\mathrm{prev}}\|_2^2\Bigr\rangle.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:refinement_neural_loss}
\end{equation}
Here $\Delta\mathbf a$ denotes the quadrotor correction or a free
cable node's correction, and $\Delta\mathbf a^{\mathrm{prev}}$
is the inherited network's output evaluated at the same current
predicted state. The brackets denote a normalized average over
the fitting windows and their simulated times, and over free
nodes for the cable, using the recording and window weights above.
Cable averages exclude times without valid
marker observations. The componentwise acceleration bound is
$a_{\max}=0.5$~m/s$^2$, and $\lambda_a=0.01$.
The factor $3$ averages the three acceleration components.
Adam updates the network weights with gradients through the
complete rollout, using learning rate $10^{-3}$ and weight decay
$10^{-4}$. Measured acceleration labels are not required.

The retained $\mathcal M_0$ has no active cable correction network.
The first update introduces this network with zero output; later
updates start from its learned weights. The zero output also serves
as the inherited correction in~\eqref{eq:refinement_neural_loss}
for the first cable fit. Thus, the first refinement
also increases model capacity, while subsequent updates retain
the same architecture.

\subsubsection{Freezing and replanning}
Each stage retains its best numerically valid training iterate.
Fitting stops under predefined training-loss plateau rules or
solver termination criteria; validation does not select parameters,
checkpoints, or stopping times. After fitting, $\mathcal M_{r+1}$
is frozen before validation. Complete command-to-tip prediction
then uses its predicted attachment motion, while measured-attachment
cable prediction remains a separate diagnostic. Complete prediction
error is evaluated after the staged fit, rather than minimized
as a joint fitting objective.

The next search uses the updated predictor with the same reference
motion, command templates, objective, and planning bounds. Each
command and its original predicted trajectory are saved before
execution. The number of physical update batches is fixed in
advance; this study uses two updates,
$\mathcal M_0\rightarrow\mathcal M_1\rightarrow\mathcal M_2$,
followed by the final evaluation.
