# Primary-source notes: dynamic cable and whip manipulation

Research checked 11 September 2026. These are bounded literature records for synthesis, not a protocol revision. Technical claims below come from primary manuscripts, publisher metadata, or author project pages. “Implication” paragraphs are analysis for the present UAV project. Numeric results across papers use different tasks, scales, tolerances, and metrology and must not be ranked directly.

## Most consequential findings

The strongest novelty pressure comes from **IRP**, **Real2Sim2Real**, **Wiggle and Go**, **Zimmermann et al.**, and **DeformX**. Collectively they already establish repeated-trial whip improvement, simulator identification from real cable motion, offline optimization followed by open-loop dynamic execution, tip targeting with speed preferences, and simulator-trained PPO whose commands are replayed on hardware. A paper cannot contribute these ingredients merely by renaming them.

The defensible distinction to investigate is the **free-flying actuator and the full command-to-cable prediction chain**: desired PVA does not equal attachment motion, so reusable vehicle-response and cable models must be updated and then tested prospectively. The evidence must show why this distinction changes prediction and task outcomes. A UAV embodiment alone is an engineering difference; controlled evidence about actuator-model necessity and reusable improvements makes it a scientific system contribution.

## 1. Chi et al.: Iterative Residual Policy

**Metadata.** Cheng Chi, Benjamin Burchfiel, Eric Cousineau, Siyuan Feng, Shuran Song. *Iterative Residual Policy for Goal-Conditioned Dynamic Manipulation of Deformable Objects*. RSS 2022; expanded IJRR **43(4), 389–404, April 2024**, first online 7 September 2023. [RSS primary PDF](https://www.roboticsproceedings.org/rss18/p016.pdf), [conference full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.00663v2), [journal DOI/metadata](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/02783649231201201), [author project](https://irp.cs.columbia.edu/).

**Verified facts.** The UR5 rope-tip target task uses a 50 cm end-effector extension and a three-parameter action primitive. IRP predicts how small action changes alter a previously observed trajectory, then samples and selects an improved action. Its delta-dynamics network is trained in simulation; hardware observations update the next trial rather than an explicit full forward simulator. Conference Sec. III–IV and Algorithm 1 specify this distinction. Sec. IV-B uses 128 action perturbations and stops hardware refinement at 2 cm error or ten iterations. Evaluation covers unseen ropes and changed robot geometry. The paper explicitly requires action repeatability. The journal adds local-minimum analysis and robustness to delta-dynamics error; publisher full-text indexing exposes those sections, but direct journal PDF access failed during this review. Conference methods were read in full; journal metadata is verified.

**Implication.** This is the mandatory closest-task comparison. Do not characterize IRP as model-free or as continuously correcting the rope during the strike: it uses learned delta dynamics and between-trial feedback. Our potential advantage is a reusable command-conditioned forward model for new plans, not iterative improvement itself. Testing another target without further fitting would strengthen that argument. A direct action-correction baseline is relevant if claiming model refinement is preferable, but a full reimplementation is not mandatory for a tightly scoped system paper.

## 2. Lim et al.: Real2Sim2Real planar casting

**Metadata.** Vincent Lim, Huang Huang, Lawrence Yunliang Chen, Jonathan Wang, Jeffrey Ichnowski, Daniel Seita, Michael Laskey, Ken Goldberg. *Real2Sim2Real: Self-Supervised Learning of Physical Single-Step Dynamic Actions for Planar Robot Casting*. ICRA 2022, pp. 8282–8289, DOI **10.1109/ICRA46639.2022.9811651**. [Final-title full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2111.04814v2), [institutional bibliographic record](https://ieor.berkeley.edu/publication/real2sim2real-self-supervised-learning-of-physical-single-step-dynamic-actions-for-planar-robot-casting/). The arXiv abstract page retains the earlier title *Planar Robot Casting with Real2Sim2Real Self-Supervised Learning*.

**Verified facts.** A robot holds one cable end and casts the other across a surface toward a distant stopping location. Physical trajectory examples tune simulator parameters using Differential Evolution; the tuned simulator produces synthetic examples, and a predictor/policy is learned using weighted simulated and physical data. Sec. IV/Fig. 2 distinguish physical tuning data, synthetic data, and final learning. Sec. V compares three simulator formulations and GP/NN function approximators. The authors report 240 physical evaluation trials across three cables, with median final errors of 8–14% of cable length. The task is planar sliding/placement rather than an airborne retained-tip strike.

**Implication.** The phrase real-to-sim-to-real describes established methodology. Cite this work directly when introducing the loop, then explain the UAV-specific setting and prospective generation evaluation. Differences in nominal/residual fitting are implementation choices unless carefully benchmarked as a method contribution.

## 3. Wang et al.: dynamic planar free-end cables

**Metadata.** Jonathan Wang, Huang Huang, Vincent Lim, Harry Zhang, Jeffrey Ichnowski, Daniel Seita, Yunliang Chen, Ken Goldberg. *Self-Supervised Learning of Dynamic Planar Manipulation of Free-End Cables*. [arXiv:2405.09581v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.09581), 28 May 2024; [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.09581v2). No final conference/journal venue was verified; cite as preprint unless independently resolved.

**Verified facts.** This is a surface-supported planar manipulation task with a weighted free end and a UR5, not airborne cable whipping. Sec. IV separates simulator-tuning real data, synthetic trajectories, and real data for predictor fine-tuning. Sec. V-D fits PyBullet parameters by minimizing mean time-aligned endpoint distance, using 60 physical trajectories and 20 additional validation trajectories per cable. Sec. VI compares trained models and analytic/GP baselines. Physical final-position errors and simulator-trajectory discrepancies are reported separately. The paper notes that its best simulator match did not yield its best final manipulation performance, with endpoint stochasticity among the suspected causes.

**Implication.** Helpful precedent for separating prediction quality from task success and recording whole-trajectory data roles. It also directly undermines an assumption that a lower model error must monotonically improve task performance. Do not use its final-position error as if it were a minimum-distance airborne hit error.

## 4. Jakobsson et al.: Wiggle and Go

**Metadata.** Arthur Jakobsson, Abhinav Mahajan, Karthik Pullalarevu, Krishna Suresh, Yunchao Yao, Yuemin Mao, Bardienus Duisterhof, Shahram Najam Syed, Jeffrey Ichnowski. *Wiggle and Go! System Identification for Zero-Shot Dynamic Rope Manipulation*. [arXiv:2604.22102](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22102), first 23 April 2026, **v2 updated 10 September 2026**. [Author site](https://wiggleandgo.github.io/) explicitly says **accepted to CoRL 2026**; proceedings publication was not independently verified. [Full v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.22102v2).

**Verified facts.** One observed planar wiggle feeds a simulation-trained temporal network predicting nine behavioral rope descriptors. These instantiate a Drake model for offline CMA-ES trajectory optimization, followed by open-loop xArm7 execution (Sec. 3). A 20 cm extender supplies reach/speed. Sec. 4 separates unseen-motion fidelity, identification ablations, and downstream striking/lobbing/draping. The optimization-based identification baseline fits the observed wiggle directly. Reported in-domain striking means are 3.55 cm for learned identification versus 15.29 cm for uninformed parameters. The 0.95 statistic is **frequency correlation**, not time-aligned positional correlation. Full lobbing success including staying is 21%, versus 54% target-hit; draping success is 63% (Sec. 4.3). Planning takes 25–120 CPU minutes (Sec. 5).

**Implication.** This is the closest overall pipeline comparison and must be discussed substantively. Our eventual claim is iterative refinement of the loaded aerial vehicle and cable together, under a different actuator/control boundary. Claiming reusable identified models requires held-out motion/target evidence, since WaG already demonstrates reuse. Its CPU runtime is not a fair direct speedup baseline for our seeded GPU planner: dynamics, hardware, task, contacts, and stopping conditions differ.

## 5. Zimmermann, Poranne, Coros: implicit-integration optimal control

**Metadata.** Simon Zimmermann, Roi Poranne, Stelian Coros. *Dynamic Manipulation of Deformable Objects With Implicit Integration*. IEEE RA-L **6(2), 4209–4216**, April 2021, DOI **10.1109/LRA.2021.3066969**. [Author manuscript PDF](https://crl.ethz.ch/papers/DynamicManipulationRAL.pdf), [ETH metadata](https://www.research-collection.ethz.ch/handle/20.500.11850/479918).

**Verified facts.** The work derives sensitivities for implicit FEM dynamics and compares batch Newton optimization with DDP. A YuMi executes planned joint trajectories open loop. Physical examples include a soft rod striking one or two targets, with an objective combining target position and high tip velocity. Sec. VI, PDF pp. 6–7, describes material calibration from physical measurements/poses and a fixed initial configuration. Sec. VII, PDF p. 8, explicitly discusses dynamic sim-to-real discrepancies from internal dissipation despite adequate quasi-static calibration. Fig. 1 shows preparatory pullback followed by a target strike; the method does not claim an online cable feedback controller.

**Implication.** Neither open-loop physics-based whipping, high-speed contact objectives, preparatory reversal, nor multiple target strikes are new in isolation. This is useful motivation for identifying dynamics using task-motion data. It does not prove that our explicit discretization is unsuitable; numerical adequacy must be evaluated for our own time steps and material regime.

## 6. Yang et al.: DeformX

**Metadata.** Yi Yang, Xiang Fei, Lehong Wang, Chenhao Li, Zilin Dai, Henry Kou, Lu Li, Howie Choset. *DeformX: A Versatile Co-Simulation Framework for Deformable Linear Objects*. [arXiv:2606.22116v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22116), 20 June 2026. [Author site](https://deformx.github.io/) says **IROS 2026 oral presentation**; treat as accepted/forthcoming based on author report, not verified proceedings. [Full paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.22116v1).

**Verified facts.** DeformX joins a Cosserat-rod simulator and Isaac Sim, with physical validation, synthetic visual data, and policy-learning demonstrations. Sec. IV-C compares the rod backend against a linked-capsule baseline. Appendix VII-D uses PPO with robot and rope-tip state in simulation; successful joint-command trajectories are replayed open loop on a physical UR5e because online rope-tip estimates are unavailable. A reported target condition has ten real executions per backend: mean minimum distance 6.6 ± 4.7 cm versus 15.1 ± 6.1 cm. Appendix VII-E separately demonstrates horizontal dynamic striking by rewarding large horizontal tip speed near the goal. That striking example is qualitative, not the quantitative benchmark.

**Implication.** Simulator-trained PPO followed by open-loop replay is already directly demonstrated. Adding our PPO result would broaden the implementation, but does not create a distinct central contribution. Its horizontal striking objective also precedes any claim that rewarding hit speed is novel. Compare the full sim-to-real behavior, not aggregate reward values.

## 7. Suresh and Atkeson: task-level ILC

**Metadata.** Krishna Suresh, Chris Atkeson. *Learning Dynamic Rope Manipulation Using Task-Level Iterative Learning Control*. **RSS 2026**, confirmed by [author project](https://flying-knots.github.io/). [arXiv:2602.21302v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21302), 13 May 2026; [full manuscript](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.21302v2).

**Verified facts.** From one human demonstration and a simplified rope model, task-level ILC updates a feedforward command across hardware trials. A quadratic-program inverse model maps measured task errors at a selected critical moment into command corrections (Sec. IV). The task is a non-planar flying knot, not point striking. Sec. V compares critical-point and uniformly weighted objectives, seven ropes, demonstration variants, command transfer, and model sensitivity. Successful commands are repeated 40 times immediately after learning; the authors explicitly distinguish this repeatability from longer-term drift. Continued updates can degrade success, and some cross-rope transfers fail within the allotted trial budget. The method principally learns commands, not a refined reusable forward model.

**Implication.** A reviewer can reasonably ask why fitting full dynamics is useful when approximate gradients can refine one command. The best answer is prospective prediction and reuse across plans/targets, rather than insisting a forward model is universally preferable. Our same-command comparisons diagnose model fidelity; new-command task tests establish utility. Keep critical-point task rewards conceptually separate from model-identification losses intended to reproduce trajectories.

## 8. Human whip evidence and preparatory motion

**Krotov et al.** Aleksei Krotov, Marta Russo, Moses C. Nah, Neville Hogan, Dagmar Sternad. *Motor control beyond reach—how humans hit a target with a whip*. Royal Society Open Science **9, 220581**, 2022, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.220581), [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9533004/). Publisher/PMC access was intermittent; the [unaltered published PDF mirrored on Wikimedia](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/fe/Motor_control_beyond_reach%E2%80%94how_humans_hit_a_target_with_a_whip.pdf) was read.

**Verified facts.** Sixteen humans manipulated a 1.6 m bullwhip. Sec. 2.6 defines physical success using target motion caused by contact, and distance error using interpolated distal whip/target segments. Sec. 3.2/Fig. 5 shows proximal-to-distal sequencing of marker-speed peaks. The authors interpret this as unfolding consistent with energy propagation, while reporting that individual speed-profile differences did not visibly track task performance. The study analyzes preparation, extension, orientation, and speed; it does not prescribe a universal curvature threshold or a robot reward gate.

**Edraki et al.** Mahdiar Edraki, Silvia Buscaglione, Rakshith Lokesh, John Peter Whitney, Alireza Ramezani, Dagmar Sternad. *Human-Inspired Robot Whip Manipulation: Preparatory Actions Increase Range and Reduce Control Effort*. **ICRA 2025 RMDO workshop**, not a main-conference paper. [Workshop record](https://deformable-workshop.github.io/icra2025/), [four-page primary PDF](https://deformable-workshop.github.io/icra2025/spotlight/01_01_05_Edraki_Human.pdf).

**Verified facts.** A planar 20-link 3D-printed whip is driven by minimum-jerk preparatory and striking segments. The optimization compares striking alone with preparation plus striking; simulations indicate greater reachable range and less modeled effort, with a Franka FR3 hardware demonstration. Sec. II-D defines minimum tip-distance error, a 1 cm hit region, and squared constraint/control-input cost. The hardware evidence is limited; avoid implying a large real statistical comparison. The preceding **BioRob 2024** paper has a different title, *Human-Inspired Control of a Whip: Preparatory Movements Improve Hitting a Target*, DOI [10.1109/BIOROB60516.2024.10719792](https://doi.org/10.1109/BIOROB60516.2024.10719792), and a 25-link model.

**Implication.** Use these sources to motivate a preparatory maneuver and explain visible distal propagation. Keep hit success a task criterion. Our wave/reversal reward is a chosen preference, not a literature-certified test that excludes every swing. Directed tip speed is a kinematic measure; it is not measured force, impulse, or impact power.

## 9. Additional useful, lower-priority references

- **Nah et al., iScience 26(8), 107395, 2023.** *Learning to manipulate a whip with simple primitive actions—A simulation study*. [Primary article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2023.107395). Optimizes low-dimensional smooth movements with impedance in a simulated arm/whip. No physical robot validation in that study. Its target rule permits any of 25 whip nodes contacting a 3 cm sphere, so it is not interchangeable with a tip-only rule. Useful for parameterization/sensitivity discussion; not a reason to copy its success definition.
- **J. Wang, X. Xiong, S. Tolu, S. N. Gorb, Journal of Bionic Engineering 21, 1761–1774, 2024.** *A Learning-based Control Framework for Fast and Accurate Manipulation of a Flexible Object*. [Primary institutional PDF](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/361777116/s42235-024-00534-2.pdf), DOI **10.1007/s42235-024-00534-2**. Combines learned/optimized movement primitives with online impedance adaptation on a two-joint arm and a lumped whip model. Sec. 3 distinguishes simulated algorithm comparisons from physical trajectory validation. Do not turn its custom percentage similarity into an RMS-error comparison.

## Recommended evidence emphasis

1. **Why the flying actuator matters.** Compare full desired-command → predicted attachment → cable against an ideal command-following attachment assumption, using identical held-out measured flights. An oracle measured-attachment rollout is an error-localization diagnostic, not a deployable baseline.
2. **Does model refinement generalize?** Freeze each generation before evaluating it on later real commands. Include at least one held-out target or motion if claiming reuse. Keep all model versions on common measurements/masks when assessing prediction.
3. **Does a refined model improve the actual task?** Execute plans generated under frozen settings and evaluate geometric miss distance, tip-only entries, and speed at actual entries. Avoid reward changes confounding generation comparisons.
4. **Is improvement repeatable?** Show all trials, session/order effects, and uncertainty at trial/session level. Immediate repetitions test execution reliability; a later session tests launch/operating drift. The two are distinct.
5. **Why full hybrid complexity?** A nominal-physics-only fit and a frozen-model planner are high-value ablations. If separately claiming drone and cable residual necessity, evaluate those removals explicitly; existing development evidence does not establish universal benefit.

No proposed comparison requires an adaptation-algorithm novelty claim. Avoid a heavyweight baseline collection without a specific claim it tests. A focused paper with strong real UAV evidence can be more coherent than adding PPO, multi-targets, new simulators, and new identification methods without adequately repeated evaluation.
